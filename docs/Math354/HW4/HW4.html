Homework 4 – Regression Consult

When people make decisions, their choices are often swayed by how information is presented. This is often described as “the framing effect.” One example of this, is how commercials describe new medications. As an example, Nereus (tradipitant), a new drug for motion-induced vomiting like sea-sickness, is marketed with a positive frame as reducing vomiting incidence from 44% to 18% (a reduction of 60%). This marketing makes it seem more effective than a negative frame that says just 18% of patients still experienced vomiting, even though both statements are true.

Xu (2024) aimed to evaluate whether older adults are more or less vulnerable to framing than younger adults. They collected a sample of 696 adults aged 18 to 71 and exposed them to three different scenarios

Scenario 1: Rate Drug Efficacy (1 = very bad, 6 = very good)

  • Positive Frame: 70 out of 100 patients felt better
  • Negative Frame: 30 out of 100 patients did not feel better

Scenario 2: Likelihood to stop drinking cocoa (1 = surely stop, 6 = surely continue)

  • Positive Frame: Stopping drinking hot cocoa reduces cadmium and cancer risk.
  • Negative Frame: Continuing to drink hot cocoa increases cadmium and cancer risk.

Scenario 3: Preference for chance choice (1 = surely safe option, 6 = surely risky)

  • Positive Frame: Choice between 200 saved vs 1/3 chance all 600 are saved.
  • Negative Frame: Choice between 400 die vs 2/3 chance all 600 die.

In each scenario, participants were randomly assigned a positive or negative frame. They rated their response on a 1-6 scale. The researchers want to build three models, one for each scenario adjusting for simultaneous linear changes in demographic variables (gender, ethnicity, education level, political affiliation).

They want to evaluate whether, as adults age, the way choices are worded have more influence on their judgments and ultimate choices. If there is a change, are those changes driven by positive or negative framings? At what age does this effect onset?

Your Goals:

  • Determine the correct analysis strategy and conduct the necessary analyses.
    • Hint 1: Ensure to assess and communicate results about the model fit.
    • Hint 2: Ensure to think about whether or which interactions need to be included.
    • Hint 3: You will need run various post hoc procedures (e.g., emmeans, emtrends or johnson_neyman).
    • Hint 4: Looking at the paper may be helpful. They do include R code, but I expect you to do a much better job using the techniques we discussed (they do somethings differently)
  • Write a clear, concise, complete, and correct report conveying an answer to their research question.

The data are in HW4Data.csv but they will require some manual and tidyverse cleaning.

1 Loading and Cleaning the Data

Code 
dat.bad = read_csv("HW4Data.csv")
dat.good = tibble(pos1 = rep("", length(dat.bad$`100 ill patients are assigned to a treatment; 70 patients felt better. - How would you evaluate the effectiveness of the drug?`) - 1))
for(i in 1:length(dat.bad)){
  dat.good = dat.good |>
    mutate(!!sym(as.character(dat.bad[1, i])) := as.vector(dat.bad[2:length(dat.bad$`100 ill patients are assigned to a treatment; 70 patients felt better. - How would you evaluate the effectiveness of the drug?`), i])[[1]])
}

dat.good = dat.good |>
  filter_out(when_any(is.na(confirmation), is.na(ageattentioncheck), is.na(attentioncheck)))

dat.good = dat.good |>
  mutate(pos1 = as.numeric(pos1),
         neg1 = as.numeric(neg1),
         pos2 = as.numeric(pos2),
         neg2 = as.numeric(neg2),
         pos3 = as.numeric(pos3),
         neg3 = as.numeric(neg3),
         age = as.numeric(age),
         pick1 = as.numeric(pick1),
         pick2 = as.numeric(pick2),
         pick3 = as.numeric(pick3)
         )

To begin cleaning the data I created a new data frame and used the first item in each column of the original data as the names of the new columns and the rest of the column as the data. Next I filtered out the failed attention checks and informed consent confirmations. After this all of the incomplete data were removed and the sample size was reduced to \(n = 600\).

diff = tibble(age = unique(dat.good$age), q1 = rep(0, length(unique(dat.good$age))), q2 = rep(0, length(unique(dat.good$age))), q3 = rep(0, length(unique(dat.good$age))))

The graphs above display the relationship between age, framing, and response. For question 1 the difference between the average response for positive framing and the average response for negative framing increases with age, signaling there may be an interaction between framing and age for drug efficacy. The same relationship is displayed for question 2 although with higher standard errors, signaling there may be the same interaction just weaker for the likelihood to stop drinking cocoa. For question 3 the interaction between age and framing is less clear, signaling there may or may not be an interaction at all for preference on chance choice.

Code 
trends.q1 = emtrends(mod.q1, "treatment1", "age")

The residual plot above shows that the errors are close to normal and their variance is centered around 0. The shape in the graph on the right seems to be due to the design of the expirement. The researchers asked participants to respond on a scale of \(1-6\), this scale is very small and discrete, which does not allow for a large variance in the responses. This may cause problems with significance later due to the limited possible responses for each question.
